Core Curriculum

Six Key Areas

From post-WWII industrial farming to soil-less vertical skyscrapers — master every paradigm covered in the CLIL exam module.

🏭

Industrial Agriculture & Monoculture

A chemically intensive system developed after WWII. Enormous single-crop farms relying on synthetic fertilizers, pesticides, and GMOs — producing high yields at serious environmental cost.

02
🌱

Sustainable Agriculture

Farming that protects the environment, public health, and animal welfare. Uses crop rotation, biodiversity, conservation tillage, and pasture-based livestock husbandry.

Crop Rotation Biodiversity Fair Wages
03
🌿

Organic Farming

Prohibits artificial fertilizers, pesticides, GMOs, synthetic hormones. Relies on compost, cover crops, green manures, and natural pest control such as beneficial insects.

Compost Cover Crops EU / USDA Cert.
04
🏙️

Urban Agriculture

Growing food inside cities — rooftop gardens, patio plots, community farms. Reduces food miles and counters the heat-island effect, though it faces challenges of land cost and soil contamination.

Food Miles Rooftop Gardens Heat Island

🔬

Greenhouse & High-Tech Farming

Controlled environments using hydroponics (liquid nutrients), aeroponics (misted roots), and vertical farming in skyscrapers — enabling year-round production with minimal resources.

Key Terminology

Essential Glossary

Master these terms before the exam. Definitions are drawn directly from the course materials.

Monoculture
The practice of growing a single crop (e.g. corn, wheat, cotton) on the same land year after year, depleting soil nutrients and requiring heavy chemical inputs.
Hydroponics
A cultivation technique using water with dissolved nutrients instead of soil. It shortens the plant growing cycle and uses less water than traditional methods.
Aeroponics
Growing plants without soil or liquid medium — roots are suspended in air and periodically misted with nutrient-laden water, maximising aeration and reducing disease.
Virtual Water
The total volume of water used throughout the entire production process of a good — "hidden" water embedded in food, textiles, and other commodities.
Agroforestry
Land-use systems where trees and shrubs are managed alongside crops or livestock. Trees sequester carbon and reduce soil erosion via hedgerows and contour planting.
Green Water
Rainwater that is stored in the soil and taken up by plants. It represents the dominant share of the water footprint of most agricultural products.
Blue Water
Surface and groundwater (rivers, lakes, aquifers) used for irrigation or industrial processing — the most visible and contested water resource in farming.
Grey Water
The volume of water required to dilute pollutants (fertilizers, pesticides) to acceptable quality standards — an indirect measure of agricultural pollution.
Peatlands
Waterlogged ecosystems that accumulate partially decomposed organic matter. Despite covering only 3% of Earth's land, they store twice as much carbon as all forests combined.
Vertical Farming
Greenhouse operations within multi-storey urban buildings. By moving food production into cities, rural lands can be reforested and topsoil restored.
Cover Crops
Plants grown between main crops to protect the soil from erosion, suppress weeds, and replenish organic matter — a cornerstone of organic soil management.
Food Miles
The distance food travels from production to consumer. Urban agriculture aims to reduce food miles, cutting transport emissions and supporting local food systems.
